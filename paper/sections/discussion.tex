\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Why search wins here.} The learned policy builds a plan in one pass of sequential decisions, and sampling
more rollouts adds diversity but no improvement of a given plan. ALNS spends the same time improving one plan with
many cheap, exact moves: our representation keeps every move feasible, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:insert} scores an insertion
in time linear in the coalition size, so a single core evaluates thousands of neighbours per second
(Table~\ref{tab:steps}). The CP-SAT-based methods search a richer neighbourhood per step but pay for building and
solving a model at every step, which is why running them in parallel helps little.

\paragraph{Implications.} We do not claim that learning is the wrong tool for multi-robot coordination: a
decentralised policy can react to events that a central plan cannot. But the static planning performance of
learned policies should be measured against strong search at matched compute, on the same cores and with the CPU
use reported. The published comparison missed plans \ConeBrlgainlo--\ConeBrlgainhi\% shorter, which a standard
operations-research technique adapted to coalitions finds at the same budget, and mostly already on one core in 2\,s,
with at most \ShareTwohi\% of the compute. Such a planner needs no accelerator and no parallel hardware, which makes
frequent replanning on a single core plausible, although our timings are on server cores and embedded processors are
slower.

\paragraph{Beyond static plans.} Real missions are dynamic: tasks are reported while the robots work, durations and
travel times vary, robots fail and radio links drop (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}a, right). The benchmark and our
confirmatory study consider the static case. In an exploratory study on development instances, we ran ALNS as a repair
loop inside a dynamic version of the benchmark's simulator, in which tasks are released over time, durations and travel
are noisy, robots fail without warning and communication has a limited range. The plan is repaired only when an event
changes the problem, that is, when a new task becomes known or a failure is detected, and not periodically or when a
duration merely deviates; each repair starts from the current plan and runs a few hundred ALNS iterations on one core.
This repair loop gave plans about 9\% shorter than the published policy run online and 8--12\% shorter than rolling
repair with CP-LNS, but a rolling greedy rule with restarts at the same compute per event came within 1.5\%, short of
the 3\% margin we had required in advance. We therefore make no claim for the dynamic case; the supplement describes
the scenarios and the results.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Static, centralised planning.} We evaluate plans for fully known, static instances, as the benchmark
  does. The RL policy is decentralised and reactive, which matters under uncertainty and with dynamic arrivals; the
  confirmatory run does not cover such settings, and our exploratory dynamic study (above) supports no claim.
\item \emph{One benchmark.} All instances come from one generator with Euclidean travel, one speed and a makespan
  objective. We expect the representation to transfer to other objectives and travel models, but have not tested it.
\item \emph{RL on CPU.} To match compute, the policy runs on the same CPU cores as every other method. On a GPU it
  could draw more rollouts. It already draws \RLNlo{} to \RLNhi{} times the published number, which makes its plans
  only \RLsamplegainlo--\RLsamplegainhi\% shorter than with ten rollouts.
\item \emph{Unknown optimality gaps.} On 50 or more tasks no method proves optimality, and the certified bounds are
  loose, so we report gaps to the best known plans and ratios between methods.
\item \emph{Tuning.} ALNS was developed and tuned on development instances of the same generator. Every competitor with a
  parameter was tuned per setting on the same instances, which favours the competitors.
\item \emph{Pre-registration.} The dry run on the validation instances was known before the freeze, the test results of the
  constructor and of the RL policy had been seen in an earlier phase, and the released test set of the benchmark
  (not blind) was used to choose CTAS-D's solver. No ALNS or CP-SAT variant and no CTAS-D port ran on the test instances
  before the freeze.
\item \emph{Single host and seed.} All timed runs used one host and one seed per instance, method and budget. On
  the validation instances, 52 cells measured on two different hosts agreed within 2\%.
\item \emph{CTAS-D.} Our port solves the published model with an open solver on a time grid of $10^{-3}$ and counts
  model building in the budget; it is not the benchmark paper's Gurobi run, although it beats that run on the
  released 20-task instances.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

On the HeteroMRTA benchmark, an adaptive large neighbourhood search over a deadlock-free coalition representation
with exact linear-time insertion needs one core for 2\,s, at most \ShareTwohi\% of the competitors' CPU time, to beat
every competitor on \Ctwoheld{} of 8 settings and the published reinforcement-learning policy on all 8. At equal
compute it finds plans \ConeBrlgainlo--\ConeBrlgainhi\% shorter than that policy and
\ConeBclasslo--\ConeBclasshi\% shorter than three CP-SAT-based methods and greedy restarts, while the exact MILP finds
a plan for only \CtasSolved{} of \CtasRuns{} instances; all of it under a pre-registered protocol. We release the solver, the experiment scripts, the pre-registration and all raw results, so that future learned
planners can be compared against this baseline at matched budgets.
