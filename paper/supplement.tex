\section{Exactness of the evaluator}
\label{sec:s-exact}

The benchmark's simulator executes pre-set routes event by event and lets an agent join a task only while the task is
not yet covered. For a plan in our representation (a global order and minimal covers), the forward pass of
Section~\ref{M-sec:repr} performs the same floating-point operations as the simulator: travel times are computed pair by
pair with the same norm, arrivals, starts and finishes in the same order. Our test suite replays hundreds of random
minimal-cover plans on all benchmark families, up to 150 robots and 500 tasks, in the simulator and requires the same
makespans (to $10^{-9}$); the plans ALNS returns are checked against the evaluator, and the incremental insertion of
Section~\ref{M-sec:insertion} against a full forward pass. Every result in the paper is the simulator's own score of the returned plan
(RL: the score of its own rollout), so an evaluator error could only have made ALNS search the wrong objective, never
report a wrong makespan.

\section{Competitors}
\label{sec:s-competitors}

All competitors run on the same pinned cores as ALNS; their budgets start once the instance is loaded and include
construction and model building. Table~\ref{tab:supp-params} lists the per-setting choices made on the development instances.

\paragraph{CP-SAT full model.} One \texttt{AddCircuit} constraint per agent over its depot and its candidate tasks (tasks it
can contribute to); a skipped task is a self-loop. Arc $j \to k$ enforces $s_k \ge s_j + d_j + \tau_{jk}$, depot arcs
bound the first start and the makespan; coverage is $\sum_i a_{ik} x_{ij} \ge r_{jk}$. Valid cuts bound the number of
members per species and task, and agents of one species are ordered by the smallest task they visit (symmetry
breaking). Times are integers on a $10^{-3}$ grid, rounded up, so every CP schedule is feasible in real time; every
plan is converted to a minimal cover and scored by the simulator.

\paragraph{CP-LNS and parallel CP-LNS.} Large neighbourhood search whose repair step is a CP-SAT solve. Each step
frees a window of tasks (along the chain that sets the makespan, close in space or time, or at random), keeps every
other coalition fixed and re-solves a small model: robots that can take a freed task get a circuit over their fixed
tasks and the freed candidates, all other robots become fixed precedence chains; the current plan is always passed as
a complete hint. CP-LNS runs one such search and gives each solver call 8 threads; parallel CP-LNS runs 8
single-threaded searches that share the best plan (makespan, then summed return times).

\paragraph{CTAS-D.} Our port rebuilds the model of the benchmark's C++/Gurobi code (planner mode
\texttt{TEAMPLANNER\_\allowbreak CONDET}): per species a continuous flow on the task graph with binary arc indicators, shared task
start times with big-M synchronisation on every used arc, cumulative capability coverage per required trait, and the
flow rounding and path split into per-agent routes. The benchmark authors' makespan objective is not in the public
code; we recovered it from their 50 shipped Gurobi solutions as the energy cost plus 100 times the makespan. Plugged
into our model, all 50 shipped solutions satisfy every row to print precision and reproduce the shipped objective
values, and their routes replay in the simulator to the shipped makespans. Deviations: species that cannot contribute
to a task get no variables (same optimum); the energy rows, which constrain nothing at the benchmark's energy cap, are
off; CP-SAT solves the model on a $10^{-3}$ time grid; the budget includes model building. Backend choice (on the
benchmark's released 20-task test set, not blind; 10 instances, 60\,s, 8 threads): CP-SAT solved 10 of 10 with plans
4.1\% shorter than the shipped 600-s Gurobi runs, HiGHS 10 of 10 at 2.2 times their makespan, SCIP 3 of 10. On the
500-task settings the port found no solution within $B_1$ on development instances and its solver held 55 and 87\,GB of memory, so it is
counted as failing there.

\paragraph{RL policy.} The released checkpoint and the benchmark's own environment, on CPU: each of 8 single-threaded
processes samples rollouts in lockstep batches (the first batch has one sample at budgets up to 10\,s, else four;
later batches fill 95\% of the remaining time at the slowest per-sample time seen so far) and the best rollout counts.
On our regenerated test instances the policy reproduces the published RL(g.) and RL(s.10) means within half a
standard deviation on all 16 settings.

\paragraph{Greedy restarts.} Eight independent runs in parallel (seeds 0--7) of our constructions: plain list scheduling, then
alternately a noisy dispatch and a random-order best insertion; the best plan any stream finished by the budget.

\section{Protocol details}
\label{sec:s-protocol}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Host.} Two Intel Xeon Platinum 8480C processors (112 physical cores) in a container with a quota of 57.6
  CPUs and 330\,GB of memory. At most six jobs of 8 CPUs at a time, one CPU per physical core, i.e.\ 48 CPUs, so the container
  stays under its quota; the only other load was a GPU keep-alive process of about 2 CPUs. RL runs on CPU.
\item \textbf{Scheduling.} Jobs of all methods are shuffled together into six slots of 8 CPUs; an 8-core job takes a
  slot, 1-core jobs of one budget are bundled 8 per slot. A slot starts a job only when at most 40\% of the container's
  CPU periods were throttled over the last second; a run that saw more than 40\% throttled periods is re-run and left
  out. On this host, 1-process jobs with at most 40\% throttled periods still received 87--100\% of their core.
\item \textbf{Code.} Every timed run used a clean checkout of a fixed commit and records it; the experiment scripts refuse a
  modified source tree, and on the test split it also refuses code whose trees differ from the frozen ones.
\item \textbf{Late runs and failures.} Pre-registered: a run that overruns its budget by more than 10\% + 0.25\,s is
  scored by the best plan its trace reached by the budget, or as a failure if it keeps no trace; a failure (no plan,
  unfinished tasks or makespan $\ge 200$) counts as 200. Late runs of the test campaign at $B_1$ on 8 cores: RL
  \LateRL, ALNS \LateALNS, others \LateOther. Scoring them by their final plans instead (descriptive), ALNS's plans are
  \Sensrlgainlo--\Sensrlgainhi\% shorter than those of the RL policy, \Senscpsatgainlo--\Senscpsatgainhi\% than
  CP-LNS's, \Senspcpsatgainlo--\Senspcpsatgainhi\% than parallel CP-LNS's, \Senscpfullgainlo--\Senscpfullgainhi\% than
  those of CP-SAT full model and \Sensconstructgainlo--\Sensconstructgainhi\% than those of greedy restarts.
\item \textbf{Statistics.} Per instance the ratio of makespans at the same budget and cores; the mean ratio with a
  paired bootstrap 95\% CI (10,000 resamples, seed 0); one-sided paired $t$-tests of the mean log ratio below 0; Holm
  within each family (equal compute: 46 tests; twice the budget: 17; one core at 2\,s: 46; called C1, C1 at $2B_1$
  and C2 in the pre-registration).
\item \textbf{Deviations from the pre-registration.} None in the confirmatory analysis. After the run, the script
  that collects the best known plans of the test split was found to read the validation results; it was fixed and
  the descriptive gaps were recomputed from the unchanged runs (recorded in the pre-registration). The late-run sensitivity above
  and every result on validation and development instances are additional and descriptive.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dynamic scenarios (exploratory, negative result)}
\label{sec:s-dynamic}

All results in this section are exploratory: they come from the development instances only (instances 0--19 of the
single-skill binary and additive settings with 50 robots and 50 tasks and of the multi-skill settings with 25 and 50
robots and 50 tasks, with the multi-skill setting with 50 robots and 200 tasks as a secondary setting), and none of them is part of the confirmatory study.

\paragraph{Simulator.} We extended the benchmark's simulator with a small set of declared changes, each covered by a
regression test: tasks stay hidden until their release time; a coalition starts as soon as the robots present cover the
task, and late or redundant arrivals leave; a failed robot halts, and its task in progress restarts once it is covered
again; the episode ends when every released task is finished and every working robot is home after the release window.
With all tasks released at time 0, no noise and no failures, the extended simulator reproduces the static one exactly.

\paragraph{Scenario families.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Noise only} (control): all tasks known at the start; moving robots stall at random (per tick, probability
  0.01 or 0.05, for 1--4 or 1--10 ticks) and task durations vary (log-normal, mean preserving, $\sigma = 0.3$).
\item \emph{Online release}: 20 tasks known at the start and 20 more every 10 time units; or half or 80\% of the tasks
  released at uniformly random times within half of the published makespan of the setting; with the noise above.
\item \emph{Release with stronger noise}: the second release process with more frequent and longer stalls.
\item \emph{Failures}: each robot fails with probability 0.1 or 0.2 at a random time within the first 70\% of the
  published makespan, stops for good, and is detected after 5 ticks without a heartbeat; realizations that make a task
  impossible are removed for all methods.
\item \emph{Limited communication} (only for the comparison of communication architectures): radio links within a range
  around the connectivity threshold of a random geometric graph, with message loss and one tick per relay hop.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Planner in the loop.} ALNS runs as a warm-started repair loop. It re-plans only on events that change the
problem: a new task becomes known, a failure is detected, or a robot abandons a task. It does not re-plan periodically
or when a duration or travel time merely deviates, because such noise only shifts the fixed schedule. Tasks that a
member has already started towards are committed and kept; each repair covers the released, uncommitted tasks. The
compute per event is 300 ALNS iterations on one core (light budget) or 1\,s on 8 cores (heavy budget); computation is
charged outside simulated time. The competitors get the same compute per event: the published policy run online (each
robot chooses its next task when it becomes free), the policy as a rollout planner, rolling CP-LNS, rolling greedy
restarts, targeted repair in the style of D-ITAGS \cite{neville2023ditags}, and a CBBA-style auction
\cite{choi2009consensus}.

\paragraph{Results.} Makespan ratios ALNS repair (light budget) / competitor, pooled over the release, noise and failure
families and the four settings: 0.860 against insertion-only repair (0.828 with failures) and 0.883 against executing
the initial plan without re-planning, so search in the loop pays; 0.914 against the published policy run online and
0.885 (light) and 0.919 (heavy budget) against rolling CP-LNS. But rolling greedy restarts at the same compute per event
reached 0.985 (95\% CI [0.981, 0.989]), targeted repair 0.991 and the auction 0.986, while our go/no-go gate, fixed
before these runs, required a ratio of at most 0.97 against greedy restarts. Re-planning at every event, noise
included, was 1\% better than re-planning only on structural events, and the heavy budget was 2.3\% better than the
light one. In the communication study, the best fixed architecture ended about 1.8 times above full information at the
shortest range, so better protocols have room to help, but our protocol did not beat a simple fixed rule, and lease
and beacon parameters changed the results as much as the architecture did. We therefore make no claim for the dynamic
case.

with one dated correction of its disclosure on AI assistance, which changes no part of the protocol. It was
